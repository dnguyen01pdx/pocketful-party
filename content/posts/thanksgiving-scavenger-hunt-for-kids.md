Every Thanksgiving has that hour. The turkey needs another 45 minutes, the grown-ups are crowded into the kitchen, and the kids have already run three laps around the couch. A scavenger hunt gives all that energy somewhere to go, and it asks almost nothing of the adults: print, hand out pencils, and point at the door.

This printable has three separate hunts, so you can pick the one that fits the weather and the crowd, or run all three across the day. There are 62 things to find in total, with big checkboxes and large type so early readers can manage on their own.

## What's in the printable

- **Page 1, Indoor Turkey Day Hunt:** 24 things to find around the house, in the kitchen and at the dinner table. Think a wooden spoon, a sock with stripes, a can with a vegetable on the label, and a dish you've never tasted.
- **Page 2, Outdoor Fall Hunt:** 22 things for the yard, a park or a walk around the block. Leaves in three colors, an acorn (bonus if it still has its cap), a pinecone, a squirrel, a spiderweb, plus four "use your senses" boxes like feeling rough tree bark.
- **Page 3, Thankful Hunt:** 16 open-ended finds and family challenges, like "find something that keeps you warm" or "find someone who helped cook and say thank you," and three blank lines for "I'm thankful for..."

Every page has a box for the hunter's or team's name and a "found" count, so you can tally up at the end if your kids like a winner. All three pages print fine in black and white.

## How to play

1. **Print one sheet per kid or per team.** For kids under 7, pair them with an older cousin or a grown-up. Teams of two work best for the outdoor hunt.
2. **Set the boundaries out loud.** Indoors, say which rooms are open. The kitchen section says "ask first" for a reason: nobody needs a seven-year-old reaching past a hot oven for the oven mitt. Outdoors, agree on where the edges are and keep everyone where an adult can see them.
3. **Explain the rule for treasures.** On the indoor hunt, kids tick the box and leave the item where it was, unless you want a "hunt table" where they bring things to show. Outdoors, give each team a paper bag for things they're allowed to keep, like leaves and pinecones. Feathers and critters are look-only.
4. **Set a timer.** 15 to 20 minutes for the indoor hunt and 20 to 30 for the outdoor one is about right. The thankful hunt has no rush and works well spread across the whole afternoon.
5. **Count it up.** One point per box. Ties go to whoever can tell the best story about one of their finds.

## Which hunt to use when

**While dinner is cooking:** the indoor hunt. It keeps kids busy and close by, and the "at the table" section has them checking the dinner table without touching anything important.

**After dinner, before pie:** the outdoor hunt. Fresh air after a big meal helps everyone, and a short walk is a good excuse for a few adults to tag along. In late November, a lot of the country gets dark early, so do this one while there's still some afternoon light.

**All afternoon:** the thankful hunt. Hand it out when people arrive and let kids work on it between other things. The family challenges get them talking to relatives they don't see often, and the oldest person in the room usually loves being asked about their favorite Thanksgiving food.

## Tips for different ages

### Ages 4 to 6

Read the items out loud and let them tick the boxes. Keep it to one section at a time, like "around the house," so the list doesn't feel huge. Non-readers can draw a quick picture of what they found next to the box instead of reading the words.

### Ages 7 to 9

This is the sweet spot. Most kids this age can read the whole sheet on their own and race a sibling. Give them the outdoor hunt in pairs and they'll come back with a bag full of leaves and a long story about a squirrel.

### Ages 10 to 12

Older kids sometimes decide scavenger hunts are for little kids. Two fixes: make them the team captain for a younger cousin, or turn the thankful hunt into a photo challenge where they take a picture of each find on a parent's phone. The family challenges, like getting a joke to land, are harder than they look.

## Ways to make it bigger

### A big crowd of cousins

With eight or more kids, split into teams with mixed ages and give each team one sheet and one pencil. Have teams start on different sections so they aren't all crowding the kitchen at once. Swap sheets at the end and let teams check each other's work.

### Rainy or cold day

Skip page 2 and run the indoor hunt twice: once as written, then a second round where everything has to be found in a different room than the first time.

### Make it a relay

Put the three pages in order and give the next page only when a team finishes the one before. The first team to finish the thankful page reads one of their "I'm thankful for" lines out loud at dinner.

## Prize ideas that cost nothing

- First pick of the pie
- The winners choose the after-dinner movie
- Sitting in the "big chair" for dessert
- Getting to break the wishbone
- Being the official taste-tester for whipped cream

## A few safety notes

The hunts are written to keep kids out of trouble, but a quick reminder helps. Keep little hands out of the kitchen while the oven and stove are on, and don't let anyone taste berries, mushrooms or seeds they find outside. If you're hunting in a park, stick together and set a meeting spot before anyone heads off.

## Save it for next year

Once the kids have played it, keep one copy in your holiday box. It works just as well next November, and the thankful page makes a sweet keepsake if you write the year on the back.
