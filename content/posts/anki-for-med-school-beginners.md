If you've opened Anki for the first time and immediately felt lost, you are definitely not alone! It looks like a simple flashcard app, but then you hear upperclassmen talking about decks, tags, unsuspending, review piles and schedulers, and it starts to feel like a second class you have to pass.

The good news is that you don't need to understand every setting on day one. Most of what people learn the hard way comes down to a few habits. Here's how I'd start if a friend asked me, built on the advice that keeps getting confirmed in well-liked r/medicalschool and r/medicalschoolanki threads and in the official Anki manual. There's a free two-page planner at the end to track your first two weeks.

## Why Anki works (the quick version)

Anki is built on two study methods with a lot of research behind them: active recall (making yourself pull up the answer) and spaced repetition (seeing a card again right before you'd forget it). A big 2013 review of learning techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful methods it compared. Anki does both automatically, so your job is mostly to show up every day. That's it!

## Day 1: set it up

Here's what the official Anki site says about the apps:

- **The computer version is free** for Mac, Windows and Linux.
- **AnkiWeb is a free sync service**, so your cards stay the same on every device.
- **AnkiDroid**, the Android app, is free.
- **AnkiMobile**, the official iPhone and iPad app, is paid, and those purchases help fund Anki's development.

Install the desktop version, make an AnkiWeb account, and sync. If you have an iPhone, you can still review on your computer or in a browser through AnkiWeb, so don't feel like you have to buy anything to start.

## Pick one main deck

This is where a lot of people get overwhelmed, so keep it simple. You'll usually choose between:

- **A big shared deck** that lots of students use, like AnKing, where you unsuspend the cards that match each lecture.
- **Your own cards** made from your lectures.
- **A mix**, which is what many students end up doing: a shared deck for the big picture plus a few of your own cards for things your school emphasizes.

Ask upperclassmen at your school what they used, since what works best depends a lot on how your exams are written. Whatever you choose, start with one main deck. You can always add more later!

## The daily routine

The single most repeated piece of advice is this: **do your reviews first, every day.** Reviews are the cards Anki thinks you're about to forget. If you skip them, they don't disappear. They pile up and show up tomorrow on top of tomorrow's cards.

A simple daily loop:

1. **Do all your due reviews** while you're fresh.
2. **Add today's new cards**, from today's lectures if you can, so learning and reviewing line up.
3. **Stop at a set time.** Rest is part of the plan!
4. **Sync before bed** so your phone and computer match.

## How many new cards a day?

Honestly, there's no magic number, and you'll see very different answers online. What everyone agrees on is that every new card you add today turns into reviews for weeks afterward, so your review load grows from your new-card number.

My suggestion: pick a number that feels a little too easy for the first two weeks, write it on the planner, and watch how long your reviews take. Then adjust. And when a week gets heavy (an exam, a family thing, being sick), **cut new cards before you skip reviews.** That one rule keeps the pile from turning into a mountain.

## A few settings worth knowing about

You really can leave most settings alone at first. Two things are good to know:

- **The daily limits** for new cards and reviews live in each deck's options. Lowering new cards is the easiest way to lighten a hard week.
- **Newer versions of Anki include FSRS**, a newer scheduling algorithm you can turn on in deck options. Lots of students like it, but it's completely fine to get comfortable with the basics first.

If you change a setting, write down what you changed and the date. The planner has a spot for this, and it saves you from wondering two weeks later why your reviews suddenly doubled.

## Cards you keep missing

Every deck has a few cards that just won't stick. Instead of hitting "Again" for the twentieth time, write the topic down and ask why you keep missing it. Do you not understand the mechanism? Are two similar cards getting mixed up? Sometimes rewatching one slide or doing a few practice questions on that topic fixes it faster than more repetitions. ## The short version

Install the free desktop app and sync with AnkiWeb, pick one main deck, do your reviews first every single day, start with a modest new-card number, and cut new cards (not reviews) when life gets busy. Give it two weeks before you judge it. You're going to get the hang of this!
