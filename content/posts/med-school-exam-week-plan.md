There's a specific feeling that shows up about two weeks before a block exam in med school. You look at the list of lectures, realize how many there are, and your brain quietly asks whether you've actually learned any of it. If that's where you are right now, take a breath! It's one of the most common feelings in first year, and it's a sign you're paying attention, not a sign you're behind.

I'm a first-year med student too, so I'm figuring out block exams alongside you. This plan is built from the advice that keeps getting confirmed in well-liked r/medicalschool threads and from learning specialists, written the way I'd explain it to a friend in my class. There's a free two-page printable at the end to map out your own fourteen days.

## Why the last two weeks need a different plan

During a normal week, the job is learning new material and keeping up with reviews. (If you want that setup, my [weekly med school study schedule](../med-school-weekly-study-schedule/) walks through it.) In the last two weeks before an exam, the job changes. You're no longer trying to see everything once. You're trying to find out what you can't do yet and fix it while there's still time.

The tool that does that best is practice questions. Flashcards tell you whether you remember a fact. Questions tell you whether you can use it when it's buried in a vignette with three other details trying to distract you. Research on learning backs this up: a well-known 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as one of the two most useful study techniques it compared, and rereading near the bottom. That's the whole idea behind this plan!

## Days 14 to 8: finish the new stuff, start questions

This first week still looks a lot like a normal week, with two changes:

- **Get through any remaining new lectures.** The goal is to have seen everything at least once by day 8. If a lecture is still sitting there unwatched a week out, it's going to stress you out every time you look at your list.
- **Start a small question block each day.** Even 10 to 20 questions on material you've already covered is plenty. Use your school's question bank or practice questions if they have them, or a board-style bank if your class uses one.
- **Keep doing your daily reviews.** This is the advice I see repeated most. Dropping your flashcard reviews for two weeks feels like it saves time, but you'll come back after the exam to a pile you can't climb out of.

It's totally normal for your first question blocks to go badly. That's actually the point! A miss now is a gift, because it shows you exactly where to look.

## Days 7 to 3: questions first, then review every miss

This is the heart of the plan. The rhythm most people recommend looks like this:

1. **Do your question block first,** while you're fresh. Timed, if you can, so it feels a little like the real thing.
2. **Review every question you missed.** Not just the right answer, but why you picked the wrong one.
3. **Write the miss down.** One line: the topic, and the reason. Did you not know the fact? Did you know it but misread the question? Did you narrow it to two and pick wrong?
4. **Go back to the source for your weakest topics.** Rewatch that one slide set or reread that one section, instead of rewatching everything.

That third step is the one people skip, and it's honestly the most useful. After a few days, your miss log starts showing patterns. Maybe it's always the same organ system, or maybe you keep changing correct answers. Either way, now you know what to fix. That's so much better than a vague feeling that you "need to study more"!

A gentle note on scores: practice scores in this stretch go up and down, and that's okay. You're aiming for fewer repeat mistakes, not a perfect percentage.

## Days 2 and 1: lighter, not harder

The last two days are where a lot of people (me included, honestly) are tempted to cram as much as possible. The advice that keeps coming up from upperclassmen is the opposite: ease off a little.

- **Do a light review** of your weak-topic list and your miss log, not brand-new material.
- **Keep your daily reviews going,** just maybe a lighter session.
- **Check the logistics:** exam time, room, what to bring, and whether your laptop and exam software are updated if your school tests on computers. Nobody wants a surprise update the morning of!
- **Stop at a set time and go to bed.** Sleep is when your brain consolidates what you learned, and an all-nighter tends to cost more than it gives.

## After the exam

Whatever happened, do something fun that night. You earned it! The next day, catch up on any reviews you let slide, then take five minutes to write down two things you'd change for the next block. That little note is how each exam gets a bit smoother than the last.

And if an exam didn't go the way you hoped, please talk to someone: your school's learning specialists, an advisor, or upperclassmen who've been through the same block. Asking for help is a normal part of med school, not a sign that you don't belong here.

## The short version

Two weeks out, finish new material and start small question blocks. One week out, put questions first and review every miss, writing down why you missed it. The last two days, go lighter, check the logistics and sleep. You've done hard things to get here, and you can absolutely do this one too!
