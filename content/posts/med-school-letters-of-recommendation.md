Asking for a letter of recommendation is one of those application steps that sounds small on paper and feels enormous in real life. You're asking someone busy to spend real time writing about you, and it's so easy to overthink the email until it's been sitting in your drafts for three weeks. If that's you right now, I promise you're not alone!

The good news is that professors and supervisors write these letters all the time, and most of them are genuinely happy to help a student they know. This is how I'd approach it if a friend asked me, built from the advice that keeps getting agreed with in premed forums and from prehealth advising offices, plus the official AMCAS details from the AAMC's 2027 Applicant Guide. There's a free two-page checklist at the end.

## First, check what your schools actually want

Letter requirements vary a lot from school to school. Some want two science professors and one non-science professor, some prefer a committee letter if your college offers one, and some cap how many letters they'll read. The AAMC's Medical School Admission Requirements (MSAR) lists each school's letter rules, so look up the schools you're interested in before you lock in your writers.

Two other things to check early:

- **Does your college have a prehealth committee?** If so, many schools expect a committee letter, and the committee usually has its own deadlines (often in the spring).
- **Does any school on your list want a specific type of letter?** Some ask for a letter from a physician or a research mentor, for example.

Most people end up with three to five writers who cover nearly every school on their list.

## Pick people who know you, not the biggest name

The advice I see repeated most, by far, is this: **a detailed letter from someone who knows your work beats a short letter from a famous name.** A professor who watched you work through problems at office hours all semester can write about how you think. A department chair who saw you once in a lecture hall of 300 can't.

Good options usually include:

- **Science professors** who saw your work up close
- **A non-science professor** who knows how you write and think
- **A supervisor** from clinical work, a job or research
- **Anyone who can share a specific story** about you, like how you handled a hard patient interaction or a failed experiment

If you're early in college, the best thing you can do right now is go to office hours more than once. That's how a professor goes from "knows your name" to "can write you a real letter."

## How to ask (and make it easy to say no)

Ask in person, at office hours, or in a kind, short email. A lot of advisors suggest phrasing it as "Would you feel comfortable writing a **strong** letter of recommendation for my medical school application?" It sounds a little awkward, but it gives them an easy, polite way to say no if they don't know you well enough. And honestly, a gentle no now is a gift compared to a lukewarm letter later!

Timing matters too. A common recommendation is to ask about **six to eight weeks** before you need the letter. If you're applying this coming cycle, asking in the winter or early spring is ideal, before professors scatter for summer travel and research.

## What to send once they say yes

This is where you can really help your writers (and your letter). Send a short packet:

- **Your CV or activity list**
- **A short note** reminding them what you did together, plus two or three things you'd love them to mention
- **Why medicine,** in a few sentences
- **Your deadline,** set a few weeks before you actually need it
- **How to submit,** which for AMCAS means the Letter Request Form (more on that below)

## How letters work in AMCAS

Here's what the AAMC's 2027 AMCAS Applicant Guide says about letters (Section 6 of the application). Always confirm the details for your own cycle:

- **You can have up to 10 letter entries** on your AMCAS application. That doesn't mean any school wants 10! Each school has its own rules.
- **Each entry is one of three types:** a committee letter, a letter packet, or an individual letter. If a letter is already inside your committee letter or packet, you don't add it again separately.
- **Writers submit through the AAMC Letter Writer Portal or Interfolio,** using the Letter Request Form you generate in your application. You don't upload letters yourself.
- **You assign letters to each school** after you've added your schools.
- **Letters aren't required to submit your application,** and you can add a new letter entry after submitting (but you can't delete or change existing entries).

## Follow up kindly, then say thank you

Writers are busy, and a reminder is totally normal. A short, friendly note about a week before your deadline is plenty. Once the letter is in, send a thank-you note, and later, when you get good news, tell them! Many writers really do love hearing how it turned out.

## The short version

Check each school's letter rules, pick people who know your work, ask six to eight weeks ahead in a way that makes saying no easy, and send a short packet with your CV and a few points to mention. In AMCAS, you can have up to 10 letter entries, and writers upload through the Letter Writer Portal or Interfolio. Follow up kindly and always say thank you. You've got people in your corner, and asking is the first step!
